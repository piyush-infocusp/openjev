KL and Soft ECE

Two terms in our training loss. One keeps the model honest about its confidence. The other keeps it close to where it started.

    1. The problem with confidence

    A model does not only give an answer. It also gives a number that says how sure it is. That number is only useful if it is honest.

    The rule is simple. If a model says "90% sure" many times, it should be right about 9 times out of 10. When this holds, we say the model is calibrated.

    Example 1: the weather forecaster

    A forecaster says "90% chance of rain" on 10 different days. If the forecaster is calibrated, it rains on about 9 of those days. If it rains on only 5, the forecaster is over-confident. If it rains on all 10, the forecaster is under-confident. In both cases you cannot trust the number.

    Example 2: the decision model

    Our model reads a support ticket and decides where it goes: billing or bugs. It also says how sure it is. Suppose it says "90% billing" on 10 tickets. Move the slider to see how many were really billing.

    Try it

    6/10

    Why does this matter to us? A ticket router can act on confidence. High confidence: route it by itself. Low confidence: ask a human. This only works if "90%" really means about 90%.

    Accuracy and calibration are different. A model can be 70% accurate and perfectly calibrated: it says "70%" and is right 70% of the time. A model can also be 95% accurate and badly calibrated.

    2. Calibration and ECE

    The reliability diagram

    To check calibration we sort predictions into bins by confidence. For example: all predictions between 0.8 and 0.9 go in one bin. In each bin we compare two numbers:

    • conf: the average confidence of the predictions in the bin.
    • acc: the share of those predictions that were right.

    If the model is calibrated, acc equals conf in every bin. The bars then sit on the dashed diagonal.

    The formula

    ECE = Σb (nb / N) · | accb − confb |

    nb is the number of predictions in bin b. N is the total. So ECE is the average gap between confidence and accuracy, with big bins counting more. Zero is perfect. Smaller is better.

    Brier is the average squared error of the probabilities: Σk (pk − yk)² per ticket, where y is 1 for the right route and 0 for the others. NLL is the average of −log of the probability given to the right route. Both are "proper" scores: they are lowest when the probabilities are honest. Watch NLL: it is lowest near T = 1.

    Why hard bins cannot be trained on

    We want to put ECE into a loss and follow its gradient. There is a catch. A prediction belongs to exactly one bin. As its confidence moves from 0.799 to 0.801, it jumps from one bin to the next. The "bin weight" is a step function. A step function has zero slope almost everywhere, so the gradient says nothing.

    Soft ECE fixes this. Every prediction joins every bin, but with a smooth weight. The weight of bin b for confidence c is a Gaussian bump around the bin centre:

    gb(c) = exp( − ((c − centreb) · bins)² )
    wb(c) = gb(c) / Σj gj(c)

    Then nb becomes the sum of weights, accb and confb become weighted averages, and the same ECE formula is used. This is exactly what soft_ece does in openjev/rlcd.py. With more bins, the bumps get narrower. Try it.

    Soft ECE is not equal to hard ECE. It is a smooth stand-in that moves the same way. Its bumps overlap, so it blurs neighbouring bins a little.

    In our code the 0/1 "was it right" flag is not differentiable. The gradient flows through the confidence conf and through the soft weights. So soft ECE pushes each confidence toward the accuracy of the bin it sits in. When the model is over-confident it pushes confidence down, and the other way round.

    3. A short history of calibration

    Dates and titles below are from memory of the literature. Where I am less sure, the line says "(unverified)".

    • 1950 Brier. "Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability", in Monthly Weather Review. A squared-error score for weather forecasts that give probabilities. This is the Brier score.
    • 1973 Murphy. "A new vector partition of the probability score" (Journal of Applied Meteorology). The Brier score splits into reliability (calibration error), resolution (how well the forecasts separate cases) and uncertainty (how hard the problem is). Calibration became one named part of forecast quality.
    • 1999 Platt. "Probabilistic outputs for support vector machines". Fit a sigmoid on a model's scores to turn them into probabilities. Called Platt scaling.
    • 2002 Zadrozny and Elkan. Isotonic regression for calibrating classifier scores. A step-wise, increasing map from score to probability. (year unverified)
    • 2015 Naeini, Cooper and Hauskrecht. "Obtaining well calibrated probabilities using Bayesian binning" (AAAI). This paper named Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) in the form we use today.
    • 2017 Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger. "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks" (ICML). Older small nets were fairly calibrated. Modern deep nets are over-confident. A one-number fix, temperature scaling, works very well. It is Platt scaling with one parameter T on the logits.
    • 2018 Kumar, Sarawagi and Jain. "Trainable calibration measures for neural networks from kernel mean embeddings" (ICML). MMCE: a calibration penalty that is differentiable, so it can be added to the training loss.
    • 2021 Karandikar et al. "Soft calibration objectives for neural networks": soft-binned calibration error used as a training objective. (authors, year and venue unverified) Our soft ECE is in the same line: smooth the bins, then train on the result.

    Where our soft ECE sits

    Brier gave us the score. Murphy showed calibration is one part of it. Naeini made ECE the standard report. Guo showed that modern nets need it. Kumar and later work turned calibration into something you can put in a loss. Our soft_ece does that with Gaussian bin weights. Our version is a simple home-made form. I do not claim it matches any one paper exactly.

    Not claimed here: any other named variant. I chose to leave them out rather than guess.

    4. KL divergence

    The intuition: extra surprise

    Say you expect the world to follow distribution q, but it really follows p. You will be surprised more often than if you had expected p. KL divergence is the average amount of extra surprise.

    KL(p ‖ q) = Σi pi · log( pi / qi )
    • It is zero when p = q. Otherwise it is positive.
    • It is not symmetric. KL(p‖q) is not KL(q‖p).
    • If qi is tiny where pi is not, the cost is huge. Expecting "never" and seeing it happen is very surprising.
    • We use natural logs, so the unit is "nats".

    Try the second button. Where q gives almost no chance to something p likes, KL(p‖q) explodes. The reverse number is different. That is the asymmetry.

    Why RL uses a KL penalty to a reference model

    In RL fine-tuning we change a model to get more reward. We start from a model that already behaves well, for example after supervised fine-tuning (SFT). We freeze a copy of it. This copy is the reference model. We then add a penalty:

    loss = −reward + β · KL( policy ‖ reference )

    The penalty is small when the policy still answers like the reference, and grows as it drifts away. It does three jobs.

    • It prevents drift and reward hacking. A reward is only a proxy. A free-running optimiser finds odd outputs that score high but are bad. Staying near the reference limits this.
    • It keeps what SFT learned. Format, language and basic skill stay in place while the policy improves on the narrow thing the reward measures.
    • It stops mode dropping. Without it, the model may collapse onto one favourite answer and give other good options near-zero probability. Our rlcd.py header gives this as the reason for the KL hold.

    There is a neat fact behind the penalty. The best policy under "reward minus β·KL" has a closed form: reference probability times exp(reward / β). So the reference is a prior, and the reward re-weights it.

    History

    • 2015 TRPO (Schulman et al.). Improve the policy but stay inside a KL trust region, so each step is safe.
    • 2017 PPO (Schulman et al.). A cheaper way to stay close: clip the probability ratio. The paper also tried a KL penalty version.
    • 2019 Ziegler et al. "Fine-tuning language models from human preferences". KL penalty from the fine-tuned model to the pretrained model, inside RLHF.
    • 2020 Stiennon et al. "Learning to summarize from human feedback". Same recipe at larger scale, with a clear picture of reward over-optimisation.
    • 2022 InstructGPT (Ouyang et al.). RLHF with PPO and a per-token KL penalty to the SFT model.
    • 2022 Gao et al. "Scaling laws for reward model overoptimization". Measures how the true quality falls as the policy drifts too far from the start. (year unverified)
    • 2023 DPO (Rafailov et al.). Uses the closed-form view above. The KL to the reference is built into the loss as an "implicit" reward, β log(policy / reference). No RL loop is needed.
    • 2024 GRPO (DeepSeekMath, Shao et al.). No value network. Rewards are compared inside a group of samples. The KL to the reference is added to the loss and is estimated with the k3 estimator (below).

    Estimating KL from samples: k1, k2, k3

    For a big language model, we cannot sum over all outputs. We sample an output x from the policy p and estimate KL from that one sample. Let r = pref(x) / p(x). John Schulman's note "Approximating KL Divergence" (2020, unverified) compares three estimators:

    k1 = −log r
    k2 = (log r)² / 2
    k3 = (r − 1) − log r
    • k1 is unbiased but noisy. A single sample can be negative, even though KL is never negative.
    • k2 is low in variance and always positive, but it is biased. It is a good approximation when p and the reference are close.
    • k3 is unbiased and always positive (since r − 1 ≥ log r). It adds a "control variate" (r − 1, which has mean zero under p) to k1, which lowers the variance.

    What our code does

    • grpo_loss in train_prism.py samples a few actions from the policy and uses k3 on each: with lr the reference log-prob and logp the policy log-prob, it computes exp(lr - logp) - (lr - logp) - 1. That is exactly r − log r − 1 with r = pref / p. The estimate is averaged and scaled by lam_kl = 0.02.
    • exact_pg_loss does not sample. Each question has only a few options, so it sums over all of them: (p * (logp - rl)).sum(). That is the exact KL(policy‖reference) with no sampling noise. It is also scaled by lam_kl.

    Both give KL in the same direction: policy first, reference second.

    5. How they combine in our loss

    loss = −reward + 0.02 · KL(policy ‖ reference) + 0.5 · softECE

    The weights are lam_kl = 0.02 and lam_cal = 0.5. In code, exact_pg_loss returns loss + args.lam_kl * kl + args.lam_cal * cal, where loss is the mean of −reward over questions.

    • −reward pushes toward the right answer. With the Brier reward, reward = 1 − Σ(p − y)².
    • KL pulls the policy back toward the frozen reference.
    • soft ECE pushes confidence toward the observed accuracy. It looks at the whole batch. calib_term needs at least 2 questions.

    Reading the push. For each term the page checks if the term gets smaller when the policy gets sharper (T down) or softer (T up). "Wants sharper" means the gradient of that term pushes toward more confident outputs. The reward term and the calibration term can disagree. That is the point of having both.

    Why Brier reward already helps, and why add soft ECE

    The Brier score is a proper score. If the true chance of option A is 0.7, the best expected reward comes from saying exactly 0.7. So maximising Brier reward already pushes toward honest probabilities. That is why our reward has a calibration effect by itself.

    It still leaves two gaps. First, the push is per question and averaged. It never looks at the group ("of all answers I gave at 0.8, how many were right?"). Second, a model that has seen its training questions can become over-confident on them, and the per-question reward does not see the gap. Soft ECE reads the batch as a group and hits the confidence-accuracy gap directly. So it is an extra push, not a replacement. It is also noisy on small batches, which is why its weight is 0.5 and not larger.

    7. Glossary

    Calibration
    When "x% sure" is right about x% of the time.
    Confidence
    The probability the model gives to its top choice.
    Accuracy
    The share of answers that are right.
    Over-confident / under-confident
    Confidence is above / below accuracy.
    Reliability diagram
    A plot of accuracy against confidence, per bin. A calibrated model lies on the diagonal.
    Bin
    A slice of the confidence range, for example 0.8 to 0.9.
    ECE
    Expected Calibration Error. The weighted average gap between confidence and accuracy over bins.
    Soft ECE
    ECE where every answer joins every bin with a smooth Gaussian weight, so it has a gradient.
    Differentiable
    Has a slope you can follow with gradient descent. A step function has none.
    Brier score
    Average squared error of the probabilities. Lower is better.
    NLL
    Negative log-likelihood. The average of −log of the probability given to the right answer. Also called log loss.
    Proper scoring rule
    A score that is best when the model reports its true beliefs. Brier and NLL are proper.
    Temperature
    A number that divides the logits before softmax. Below 1 sharpens. Above 1 flattens.
    Logits
    The raw scores before softmax turns them into probabilities.
    KL divergence
    The average extra surprise when you expect q and the world is p. Not symmetric.
    Nat
    The unit of information when you use natural logs.
    Reference model
    A frozen copy of the model from before RL. The policy is kept near it.
    Policy
    The model being trained. It maps a question to a distribution over answers.
    SFT
    Supervised fine-tuning. Training on labelled examples before RL.
    Reward hacking
    Getting a high reward in a way that does not match what we really want.
    Mode dropping
    The model keeps one favourite answer and gives other good ones near-zero probability.
    Trust region
    A limit on how far one update may move the policy. TRPO uses a KL limit.
    k1, k2, k3
    Three ways to estimate KL from samples. Ours uses k3: r − log r − 1 with r = p_ref / p.
    lam_kl, lam_cal
    The loss weights for the KL term (0.02) and the soft ECE term (0.5).
    GRPO
    Group Relative Policy Optimisation. RL that compares rewards inside a group of samples, with no value network.
